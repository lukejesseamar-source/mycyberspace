On Building in Public

February 2026 · 6 min read

There's something terrifying about showing unfinished work to the world. Every instinct screams to wait—wait until it's perfect, wait until you've figured it all out, wait until you can present something polished and complete.

But perfection is a moving target. The longer you wait, the higher the stakes become, and the harder it gets to actually ship anything. I've watched this pattern play out in my own work countless times: the project that stays forever in draft mode, the essay that never quite feels ready, the code that could always be just a little cleaner.

The Trap of Waiting

The problem with waiting for perfection is that you're optimizing for the wrong thing. You're trying to minimize embarrassment rather than maximize learning. You're protecting your ego instead of developing your craft.

Building in public flips this equation. Instead of hiding until you've got it all figured out, you make the mess part of the process. You show the rough drafts, the failed experiments, the half-baked ideas. And in doing so, you give yourself permission to be a beginner.

What I've Learned

Since committing to building in public, I've noticed a few things. First, people are far more forgiving than I expected. The audience I imagined—critical, judgmental, waiting to pounce on every mistake—doesn't really exist. Most people are either supportive or indifferent, and both are fine.

Second, sharing work early creates accountability. When you tell people what you're working on, you're more likely to actually finish it. The weight of public commitment, even to a small audience, is real.

Third, feedback comes when you need it most. If you wait until something is "done" to share it, you're often too invested to make major changes. But if you share early, while the work is still malleable, people's input can actually shape the final outcome.

The Practice

Building in public isn't about constant self-promotion or performative productivity. It's simpler than that: it's just showing your work. Write the essay and publish it, even if it's rough. Build the tool and put it online, even if it's buggy. Make the thing and let people see it.

The goal isn't to be perfect. The goal is to be present, to participate, to learn in the open. And over time, this practice compounds. Each imperfect thing you share makes the next one a little easier. The fear doesn't disappear, but it becomes manageable.

So this is me, building in public. This site, these essays, whatever projects come next—none of it will be perfect. But it will be real, and it will be mine, and that's enough.
